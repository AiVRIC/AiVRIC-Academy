Assurance: testing, evidence and audits

Design versus operating effectiveness, what makes evidence audit-ready, how SOC 2 Type I and Type II differ, and handling exceptions and corrective action plans.

Practice: 3 activities

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Tell a test of design from a test of operating effectiveness
  • Judge whether evidence is audit-ready, and strengthen weak evidence
  • Explain how SOC 2 Type I and Type II reports and ISO 27001 certification audits work
  • Turn a finding into a tracked corrective action and validate the fix

1What assurance is, and who gives it

A control written in a policy is a promise. Assurance is the work of checking whether the promise is kept: an objective examination that gives someone, from a control owner to the board or a customer, confidence that a control works as intended.

Assurance comes in levels. Each is more independent of the people running the control than the one before, and independence is what makes a conclusion worth relying on.

Management self-assessment

Control owners check their own controls on a schedule. Cheap and frequent, but the people checking are the people being checked.

Second-line monitoring

Security, risk or compliance functions test the first line's controls and challenge owners. More objective, though still part of management.

Internal audit

The third line: independent of management, reporting to the board or its audit committee, and planning its audits around risk.

External audit or assessment

An independent firm or body examines controls against a standard and issues a report or certificate outsiders can rely on.

This lesson follows Big Venture Incorporated, a fictional freight-software company with about 600 staff, as it moves from a SOC 2 Type I report to its first Type II.

2Design versus operating effectiveness

Every control test answers one of two questions, and auditors keep them apart.

Test of design

Would the control, as designed, meet its objective? The tester walks one instance through from start to finish and asks whether it addresses the risk and would catch the problem it exists for.

Test of operating effectiveness

Did the control actually operate, consistently and as designed, across a defined period? The tester checks instances from across the period against the control description.

Design comes first: there is no point sampling a control that could not work. A quarterly access review that skips contractors and service accounts can run flawlessly every quarter and still fail on design.

Example

Big Venture's offboarding control says IT disables a leaver's accounts within one business day of the HR termination date. On design it passes. On operation, the tester compares termination dates with deactivation times for a sample of the year's leavers, and finds three accounts that stayed active for a week.

3How controls are tested

Testers combine four methods, roughly from least to most convincing.

  • Inquiry: asking the people who run the control how it works.
  • Observation: watching it performed, such as an approval gate stopping an unapproved deployment.
  • Inspection: examining the records the control produced: tickets, approvals, logs, configurations.
  • Re-performance: running the control independently and comparing results, such as working out from HR records which accounts should have been disabled, then checking the identity system.

Watch out

Inquiry alone is not evidence that a control operated. An owner's honest account of what usually happens says nothing about what happened on a given day, so auditors corroborate it with one of the other methods.

Sampling. Most controls run too often to check every instance. The population is every instance in the period under review, such as every production change or every leaver. The sample is the subset the tester inspects. The period is the window the conclusion covers.

Three principles matter more than any table: the more often a control runs, the more instances get tested; the population must be complete; and the tester, not the owner, picks the sample, spread across the whole period, so nobody can steer it toward the good months.

4Evidence that stands up

Evidence turns "we do this" into "here is proof we did". Audit-ready evidence is:

  • Complete: every instance in scope, not just the ones that went well.
  • Accurate: it reflects what happened, from a report or query that can be shown to be correct.
  • Dated: it shows when the control operated, not only when someone captured it.
  • Attributable: it shows who performed, approved or reviewed the work.
  • From the system of record: taken from the tool where the work happens, not retyped.
  • Retained: kept, unaltered, as long as the audit, contract or law requires.
  • Covering the whole period: no unexplained gaps in the window being tested.
ControlWeak evidenceStrong evidence
Quarterly access reviewAn email saying the reviews are doneAn identity-system export of every account, each reviewer decision, the sign-off date and removal tickets
Change approvalAn undated, cropped screenshot of one approved changeEvery deployment in the period from the pipeline, each linked to its approval with approver and timestamp
OffboardingA hand-edited spreadsheet of leavers marked "done"The HR termination report matched to deactivation logs, with the time between them for each leaver
BackupsA policy stating backups run nightlyJob logs for the period and a dated restore test with its result and who ran it

Continuous collection beats the scramble. Big Venture prepared its Type I by chasing screenshots in the weeks before fieldwork, finding gaps too late to fix. For the Type II period, evidence is captured as each control runs, and the compliance team checks a sample each month. Gaps now surface in the month they open.

Integrity. An auditor needs to know a record could not have been quietly edited afterwards. Strong programs rely on tamper-evident records: logs ordinary users cannot alter, append-only audit trails, and storage where changes are themselves logged. An audit trail shows who did what, and when.

5External assurance: how SOC 2 and ISO 27001 audits work

Lesson 3 described what SOC 2 and ISO 27001 are. Here the focus is how their audits run.

A SOC 2 report is issued by an independent CPA firm that examines a service organization's controls against the Trust Services Criteria and gives its opinion. It comes in two types.

Type I: design at a point in time

Whether the system description is fair and the controls were suitably designed as of a specific date. Often a first report, but silent on how controls performed over time.

Type II: design and operation over a period

Also whether the controls operated effectively throughout a review period, commonly several months to a year, tested with the methods and sampling above.

A Type II report lists each test performed and its result. Where a control did not operate as described, it records an exception, also called a deviation. Readers weigh which controls exceptions touch and how management responded. Serious or widespread failures can lead the auditor to qualify its opinion.

Because a report covers a fixed period, customers reading it months later ask what has happened since. A bridge letter from the organization's management, not the auditor, states whether there have been material changes since the period ended. It carries only management's word.

For ISO 27001, an accredited certification body audits the management system against the standard in two stages. Stage 1 reviews design and documentation, such as the scope, risk assessment and Statement of Applicability, and judges readiness. Stage 2 tests whether the system is implemented and working. Certification runs on a three-year cycle, with surveillance audits at least annually and a recertification audit before the certificate expires. Major nonconformities must be resolved to the certification body's satisfaction before a certificate is granted.

Definition

An attestation, such as a SOC 2 report, is an independent opinion on controls within a stated scope and period. A certification, such as ISO 27001, confirms a management system conformed to a standard when audited. Neither says an organization is secure.

6Findings, exceptions and corrective action

  • Severity: rate each finding by the risk it leaves open, on a scale defined in advance. A missed review on a system holding customer data outranks a late training reminder.
  • Root cause: ask why until the answer is something you can change. "IT forgot" is a symptom; "deactivation waits on a ticket HR raises by hand" is a cause.
  • Correction and corrective action: a correction fixes the instance (disable the accounts today); a corrective action removes the cause (feed HR terminations straight into the identity system). Both are needed.

Findings are tracked in a corrective action plan. US federal programs call it a plan of action and milestones (POA&M), a format now used well beyond government.

Closing the loop. An item closes when someone other than the owner validates the fix, not when the owner says it is in. An operating failure needs the fixed control to run long enough to build a new population, which is then sampled as before. Big Venture's offboarding fix went live in February; in May the second line tested every leaver since, found each disabled within the day, and closed F-01 with that evidence attached.

Watch out

Rewriting a control to match what happened, such as changing "one business day" to "ten", is not a fix. It changes the risk carried, so it is a decision for the risk owner to make and record.

7In practice: test one control yourself

  1. Pick a control that runs often, such as change approval or offboarding. Write down its objective and the evidence it should leave.
  2. Test the design. Walk one instance through. Would it catch the problem it exists for, including contractors and emergency changes?
  3. Build the population for the last quarter from the system of record, and note how you know it is complete.
  4. Sample and inspect instances spread across the quarter. Record every exception.
  5. Grade the evidence against the seven properties. What would it take to capture the missing ones as the control runs?
  6. Write one POA&M entry for the worst gap, including how the fix will be retested.

8Key terms

Test of design
A check of whether a control, as designed, would meet its objective.
Operating effectiveness
Whether a control ran consistently and as designed across a defined period.
Population and sample
Every instance of a control in the period under review, and the subset a tester inspects.
Exception
An instance where a tested control did not operate as described; in SOC 2, also a deviation.
Bridge letter
A management letter covering the time since a SOC 2 report's period ended.
Surveillance audit
A periodic certification body audit between ISO 27001 certification and recertification.
POA&M
Plan of action and milestones: tracked findings, each with a linked control, owner, dated milestones and status.
