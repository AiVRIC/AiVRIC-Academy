What GRC is, and why it exists

Three questions, one system: what governance, risk and compliance each answer, who does what across the three lines, and why fragmented programs fail.

Practice: 3 activities

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Define governance, risk and compliance by the question each one answers
  • Recognize the costs of a fragmented program
  • Describe the roles in the IIA Three Lines Model
  • Check a program against five signs of maturity

1Three questions, one system

GRC stands for governance, risk and compliance. It is tempting to treat them as three departments with three spreadsheets. They are better understood as three questions asked of the same organization, every day:

Governance

Who decides, and what have we committed to?

Direction and accountability: the objectives security serves, who owns which decisions, and the policies and standards the organization has signed up to.

Risk

What could go wrong, and is that acceptable?

Exposure and judgment: what could stop the organization reaching its objectives, how likely and how bad, and whether the remaining exposure sits inside what leadership agreed to carry.

Compliance

What are we required to do, and can we show it?

Obligation and proof: the laws, regulations, contracts and frameworks that apply, and the evidence that the organization does what it says.

Each question depends on the others. Policies written without a view of risk protect against the wrong things. Risk decisions made without governance have no owner and no record. Compliance without either becomes a checklist: work done to pass an audit rather than to run the organization well.

Definition

GRC is the coordinated way an organization sets direction for security and risk, decides how much risk to carry, and demonstrates that it meets its obligations, using one shared set of controls, risks, decisions and evidence.

2Why GRC exists

Very few organizations set out to build a GRC program. They arrive at one because the questions start arriving from outside:

  • Customers send security questionnaires and ask for a SOC 2 report or an ISO 27001 certificate before they sign.
  • Regulators set obligations (HIPAA for health data, GDPR for personal data in Europe, sector rules in finance and defense) and investigate when something goes wrong.
  • Boards and investors ask whether the organization is carrying more risk than it agreed to, and who is accountable for it.
  • Insurers and partners want evidence that basic controls are in place before they extend cover or connect systems.

The first answer to each question is usually a project: a spreadsheet for the questionnaire, a consultant for the audit, a slide for the board. The second and third time, the cost of answering from scratch becomes obvious. GRC is what an organization builds when it decides to answer these questions once, from a system, instead of again and again from memory.

3What fragmentation costs

A common pattern in growing organizations: one team prepares for SOC 2, another owns ISO 27001, a third answers HIPAA questions, and risk lives in a separate register that nobody connects to any of them. Each keeps its own control list, its own evidence folder and its own reporting. The symptoms are predictable.

The same control, many times

Multi-factor authentication appears in every framework under a different name and is tested, evidenced and reported separately for each one.

Evidence that goes stale

Screenshots collected for last year's audit say nothing about today. Gaps surface when an auditor finds them, not when they open.

Risk with no owner

A risk is "accepted" in a meeting, with no record of who decided, why, or when the decision should be looked at again.

Reports that disagree

Leadership sees one framework's status at a time, and two teams can give two different answers to the same question.

Integrated GRC fixes the structure rather than the symptoms. The organization keeps one set of controls, mapped to every obligation it has. Risks point at the controls that reduce them. Decisions are recorded with their owner and rationale. Evidence is collected once and reused wherever the same control is asked about.

4Who does what: the Three Lines Model

GRC is a shared responsibility, and the most widely used way to describe who carries which part is the Institute of Internal Auditors' Three Lines Model, published in 2020 as an update of the older "three lines of defense". It describes roles, not departments: one person can hold more than one.

Governing body

Accountable to stakeholders for the organization's oversight. Sets direction, approves risk appetite and holds management to it. In most companies, the board or a committee of it.

First line

Management that delivers products and services and owns the risks that come with them. Engineering, IT and operations teams who run controls day to day sit here.

Second line

Expertise, support, monitoring and challenge on risk-related matters. Security, risk and compliance functions write standards, maintain the risk register and check that the first line's controls work.

Third line

Internal audit: independent and objective assurance and advice to the governing body on whether governance and risk management are working. Independence from management is what makes this line useful.

External auditors and regulators sit outside the model but rely on it. When an external auditor asks who owns a control, who reviews it and who checked the review, they are asking the three lines question.

5What good looks like

A mature GRC program is less about the size of its documentation and more about whether a few simple statements are true. Use them as a quick health check on any program, including your own:

  • Every control has a named owner, and the owner knows it.
  • Every significant risk ends in a decision: treated, accepted, transferred or avoided, with who decided, why and until when.
  • Evidence is collected as the work happens, not assembled the week before the audit.
  • One control answers every framework that asks about it.
  • Leadership sees one picture of the program, and two people asked the same question give the same answer.

6In practice: map your current program

Take thirty minutes with whatever you have today, even if it is a folder of spreadsheets.

  1. List your obligations. Every framework, regulation and contract clause you are expected to meet, and who owns each one.
  2. Find the duplicates. Pick five controls that appear in more than one obligation (access reviews, MFA, backups, logging, vendor reviews). How many times is each one evidenced?
  3. Trace one risk. Choose a risk from your register and follow it: which controls reduce it, who decided the remaining exposure is acceptable, and where is that written down?
  4. Write one sentence describing what an integrated program would change for you. That sentence is the goal the rest of this course works toward.

7Key terms

Obligation
Anything the organization must or has agreed to meet: a law, regulation, framework requirement or contract term.
Control
A safeguard or practice that reduces risk or meets an obligation, such as an access review, encryption at rest or a change approval.
Risk appetite
The amount and type of risk the organization is willing to pursue or retain in pursuit of its objectives, set by its leadership.
Assurance
An objective examination that gives confidence a control or process works as intended, from management self-checks to an independent audit.
Evidence
Records that show a control operated: logs, tickets, approvals, reports and configurations, dated and attributable.
