Beyond the walls: third parties, incidents and change

Third-party risk across the vendor lifecycle, how incidents feed back into risk and controls, and keeping a program current as the business changes.

By the end of this lesson you can

  • Run third-party risk across the vendor lifecycle, in proportion to each vendor's tier
  • Read an independent report for its scope, period and exceptions
  • Feed an incident back into the risk register, controls, policy and metrics
  • Recognize business changes that should reopen scope and risk assessment

1You can outsource the work, not the accountability

Every organization relies on others: cloud hosting, payroll, email, support software. Each is a third party, an outside organization whose service may touch your data, your systems or your operations.

Take Big Venture Incorporated, a fictional company of about 600 people that sells freight-management software. Its platform runs on a cloud provider, its support team works in a hosted ticketing tool, and a small firm prints and mails tax forms. Employee names, bank details and tax identifiers flow to all three.

  • Accountability stays with you. Customers and regulators look to the organization that collected the data. Privacy and sector rules commonly require a written contract with vendors that handle regulated data, and hold you responsible for overseeing them.
  • Data and access flow outward. A vendor with a copy of your data or a login to your environment is part of your attack surface, whether or not it appears on your network diagram.
  • Dependence is a risk of its own. If a critical vendor goes down, your service goes down with it.

Vendors have vendors. A fourth party is a third party's own supplier, such as the cloud provider behind your ticketing tool. You rarely have a contract with it, so you manage it through the third party: ask which subcontractors handle your data and require notice when that list changes.

Definition

Third-party risk management is identifying, assessing, contracting for and monitoring the risks of relying on outside organizations, in proportion to how much each one matters, from before the contract is signed until after it ends.

2The third-party risk lifecycle

Third-party risk is managed across the whole relationship, not only at purchase. Monitoring can send a vendor back to due diligence, and offboarding closes the record the inventory opened.

Inventory

Every vendor, its owner, the data it receives and the access it has. Expense reports and single sign-on logs find the ones nobody registered.

Tiering

Rank each vendor by criticality to operations and by the data and access it holds. The tier sets how much scrutiny follows.

Due diligence

Before the contract, assess security with a questionnaire, independent reports and external signals, at a depth set by the tier.

Contract

Security requirements, prompt notice of a breach, a right to audit or to receive assurance reports, limits on subcontracting, and data return or deletion at the end.

Ongoing monitoring

Reassess on the tier's cadence, review each new report, watch for incidents and material changes, and track findings to closure.

Offboarding

Revoke accounts and keys, get your data back or written confirmation it was deleted, and update the inventory.

A simple tiering model at Big Venture:

TierWhat puts a vendor hereBig Venture exampleDiligence and monitoring
CriticalSensitive data or production access, or an outage stops the serviceThe cloud provider hosting the payroll platformFull questionnaire, report review, contract security terms; yearly
ModerateSome sensitive data or internal access, with workarounds if it failsThe support ticketing toolShort questionnaire, report if one exists; every two years
LowNo sensitive data, no access, easy to replaceA font license for marketingBasic contract and ownership checks at renewal

A tier is not permanent. When the printing firm began receiving full bank details, its tier should have changed too.

Watch out

Tiering by spend. A free tool with broad production access can be riskier than the largest invoice. Tier by data, access and dependence.

3Reading the evidence a vendor gives you

For critical vendors, the strongest evidence is an independent report, such as a SOC 2 report or an ISO 27001 certificate: someone outside the vendor checked. Both are easy to misread.

Scope

Does it cover the service you are buying? A report on corporate IT says little about the product holding your data. Read the system description or the certificate's scope statement.

Period

A SOC 2 Type II report covers a period that has already ended; for the gap since, ask for a bridge letter. A certificate has issue and expiry dates and is kept current by surveillance audits.

Exceptions

The auditor lists tests where a control did not operate as described. Ask what the vendor did about each, and decide whether it matters for your data.

What is left to you

Complementary user entity controls are the ones the vendor assumes you run. The report also shows whether its key suppliers were tested or carved out.

Questionnaires fill in what reports miss, but they are the vendor's own answers. Ask for evidence on the points that matter most, and reuse one question set so answers compare.

External security ratings score a vendor from what is visible on the internet: exposed services, certificate hygiene, leaked credentials. They help spot changes between reviews. They cannot see how a vendor handles your data inside its systems, and can include assets it does not own. Treat a rating as one input, never the answer.

Due diligence ends like every risk activity in this course: in a decision. Approve, approve with conditions, or decline, recorded with who decided and when to revisit.

4Incidents: when the program meets reality

An incident is the moment a risk on the register stops being hypothetical. The GRC question is whether the program learns from it. Most organizations describe response in four phases, consistent with NIST's incident handling guidance:

  • Prepare. Roles, playbooks, contacts, logging and tools, agreed before they are needed.
  • Detect and analyze. Recognize that something is wrong, establish scope and severity, and record what is learned as it is learned.
  • Contain, eradicate and recover. Stop the spread, remove the cause and restore normal operation.
  • Post-incident activity. Review what worked and what did not, and turn the findings into changes.

The last phase is where GRC takes over. Suppose Big Venture's ticketing vendor reports that an attacker used a stolen session to export attachments, some of them customer shipping records. The follow-up should reach every part of the program:

Risk register

"Vendor data leak" was scored unlikely, and it has happened. Re-rate it and check whether the residual risk is still inside appetite.

Corrective actions

Each gap becomes a dated, owned action: shorter session timeouts, no customer records in tickets, limits on attachment retention.

Policy and vendors

Update the data handling standard, retier the vendor if its role has grown, and check the contract: did notice arrive as promised?

Metrics

Track time to detect, time to contain and closure of actions, so leadership can see whether the next one goes better.

Watch out

Notification obligations. Laws, regulators and contracts may require you to inform customers, individuals or authorities within set timeframes, and the clock can start when you learn of an incident, including one at a vendor. Triggers and timelines vary, so legal and compliance should review every significant incident early.

5Change: keeping the program current

A GRC program describes the organization as it was when someone last looked. Every significant change makes part of it wrong: scope that misses a system, risks never assessed, controls mapped to an environment that is gone. The triggers are predictable:

  • New products and markets. New data types, new obligations and new frameworks customers ask about.
  • Acquisitions. The acquired company brings its own systems, vendors and gaps. Its controls belong in the common control set, not beside it.
  • Cloud migrations. Some controls shift to the provider and new configurations become yours, so owners and evidence sources change.
  • New regulations. New obligations to map against existing controls, with the gaps planned and owned.
  • New technology, such as AI. Questions about where company data goes, who sees the outputs and which new rules apply.

The answer is not a bigger annual review. It is one question built into existing processes such as procurement, architecture review and product launch: does this change our scope, our risks or our controls? If so, the assessment reopens before the change ships.

Example

Big Venture plans a feature that uses a third-party AI model to flag unusual shipment patterns. One change touches the whole course: a new critical vendor to tier, customer data flowing somewhere new, a risk assessment for wrong or leaked outputs, contract terms on whether the provider may train on customer data, and emerging AI rules to map.

6Putting it together

This course began with three questions. They are also a fair test of this lesson.

Governance

Who decided?

Someone owns every vendor relationship, every post-incident action and every accepted change in risk, and the record says who.

Risk

What could go wrong?

The register reflects vendors, past incidents and recent changes, not only the risks obvious when it was first written.

Compliance

Can we show it?

Due diligence, contracts, incident reviews and closed actions leave evidence an auditor, customer or board can follow.

The five signs of maturity from the first lesson still hold, stretched past the organization's own walls:

  • Every control has a named owner, including the ones a vendor runs for you and the ones it expects you to run.
  • Every significant risk ends in a decision, vendor approvals and re-ratings included.
  • Evidence is collected as the work happens, from each year's vendor report to the incident timeline.
  • One control answers every framework, and a new regulation or acquisition is mapped into it.
  • Leadership sees one picture, and it includes third parties, incidents and change.

7In practice: test the program beyond your walls

Take forty-five minutes with your vendor list and risk register.

  1. Tier five vendors. Pick five from your inventory or last quarter's invoices, note each one's data and access, and assign a tier. Does the scrutiny each gets match its tier?
  2. Read one report properly. For your most critical vendor, write down the scope, the period or expiry date, any exceptions, and the controls it expects you to run. Who on your side runs them?
  3. Trace one incident. Choose a recent incident or near miss. Which risk did it touch, was it re-rated, and where are the follow-up actions tracked?
  4. Plant one trigger. Find one existing process where a single question about scope, risk and controls would catch change early. Write the question and name who answers it.

8Key terms

Fourth party
A third party's own supplier or subcontractor, usually managed through the contract with the third party.
Vendor tiering
Ranking vendors by criticality, data and access so that due diligence and monitoring match the risk each one carries.
Bridge letter
A statement from a service organization's management covering the time since its last report period ended.
Complementary user entity controls
Controls a service organization assumes its customers operate, listed in its SOC 2 report.
Post-incident review
A structured look back at an incident, ending in owned, dated changes to risks, controls and policy.
