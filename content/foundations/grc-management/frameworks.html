1Must, agreed, chosen

Every compliance program starts with a list of things the organization is expected to meet. The names look alike but carry very different weight, so first sort each one by where its authority comes from:

Laws and regulations

What must we do, whatever we prefer?

Set by governments, enforced by regulators with investigations and penalties. Examples: the HIPAA Security Rule, the GDPR.

Contractual obligations

What did we sign up to?

Binding because the organization agreed to them, enforced by the other party. Examples: PCI DSS, which reaches merchants through the card brands and their banks; security terms in customer contracts.

Standards and frameworks

What do we use to organize and prove our work?

Voluntary in law, adopted by choice, often because customers ask. Examples: ISO/IEC 27001, SOC 2, NIST CSF.

Take a fictional 350-person company selling a patient scheduling platform to clinics in the US and Europe. Handling health data for US clinics makes it a business associate, so the HIPAA Security Rule applies to it directly. Processing patient data for European clinics brings the GDPR. Taking card payments brings PCI DSS through its bank. Larger customers' contracts add terms such as breach notice deadlines and annual penetration tests.

None of that is optional. Then come the frameworks, in three kinds:

  • Certifiable standards, such as ISO/IEC 27001, where an accredited body audits the organization and issues a certificate.
  • Attestation frameworks, such as SOC 2, where an independent auditor examines the organization's controls against published criteria and issues a report with an opinion.
  • Voluntary frameworks and catalogs, such as NIST CSF, NIST SP 800-53 and CIS Controls, used to structure the program. Nothing is issued.

Definition

A framework is a published, structured set of requirements, outcomes or controls that an organization uses to build, measure or demonstrate its security program. A framework only becomes mandatory when a law, regulation or contract makes it so.

2ISO/IEC 27001: a certifiable management system

ISO/IEC 27001, published jointly by ISO and IEC and currently in its 2022 edition, specifies the requirements for an information security management system (ISMS). It certifies how an organization manages security, not a product or a set of technical settings.

The standard has two parts that work differently:

  • Clauses 4 to 10 are the management system requirements, and all of them apply: context and scope, leadership, planning (including risk assessment and treatment), support, operation, performance evaluation and improvement.
  • Annex A is a reference list of controls. The organization checks its risk treatment against it and records which controls apply, and why, in a Statement of Applicability.

The 2022 edition reorganized Annex A into 93 controls in four themes:

Organizational

Policies, roles, assets, suppliers, incident management, continuity and legal requirements.

People

Screening, terms of employment, awareness and training, and duties after someone leaves.

Physical

Secure areas, entry controls, equipment protection, clear desk and secure disposal.

Technological

Authentication, access rights, malware protection, logging, backup and secure development.

A companion standard, ISO/IEC 27002, is guidance on each Annex A control: its purpose and typical implementation. Organizations certify against 27001, never 27002.

Certification is done by a certification body that has itself been accredited to audit against the standard. The certificate covers a defined scope, such as one product and the teams that run it, and is kept current by periodic surveillance audits and recertification. On someone else's certificate, read the scope statement first.

3SOC 2: an attestation, not a certificate

SOC 2 is an examination framework from the AICPA, the US professional body for CPAs. A licensed CPA firm examines a service organization's system description and controls against the Trust Services Criteria and issues a report with its opinion. There is no certificate and no pass mark: the deliverable is the report, usually shared with customers under a nondisclosure agreement.

The criteria are grouped into five categories, and the organization chooses which are in scope:

  • Security is always included. Its criteria, known as the common criteria, form the core of every SOC 2 report.
  • Availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy are optional, added when customers rely on them: availability for a hosting service, processing integrity for a payroll processor.

The criteria describe what must be achieved, not how. Each organization designs its own controls to meet them, so two SOC 2 reports can look very different. How the examination works, including Type I and Type II reports, is lesson 6.

Watch out

Neither a SOC 2 report nor an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate shows that an organization meets HIPAA, the GDPR or PCI DSS. They overlap a great deal, but each law and contract has requirements of its own.

4NIST CSF, NIST SP 800-53 and CIS Controls

The voluntary frameworks issue nothing. They give a program its structure and vocabulary without an auditor's schedule attached.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 describes security as outcomes, not controls, and is written for organizations of any size or sector. It is organized into six functions, each broken into categories and subcategories:

  • Govern: strategy, expectations, roles and policy, new as a function in version 2.0.
  • Identify: understanding assets, risks and where to improve.
  • Protect: safeguards that manage risk.
  • Detect: finding and analyzing possible attacks and compromises.
  • Respond: acting on a detected incident.
  • Recover: restoring operations after an incident.

Organizations use the CSF to describe a current and a target profile; the gap between them becomes a roadmap.

NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5 is a detailed catalog of security and privacy controls, grouped into families such as access control, incident response and supply chain risk management, with enhancements that add rigor. It is mandatory for US federal information systems, with baselines published in SP 800-53B, and widely used elsewhere as a model of a well-specified control.

CIS Controls v8, from the Center for Internet Security, is a prioritized set of 18 controls, each broken into specific safeguards. Its Implementation Groups set the order: IG1 is the essential baseline for every organization, IG2 adds safeguards for more complex environments, and IG3 adds the rest for those facing sophisticated attackers.

Example

The scheduling company uses CIS IG1 to decide what to fix first, reports to its board in the six CSF functions, and borrows 800-53 wording when it writes controls. None is audited; all three shape the control set the audits examine.

Choosing is less about which framework is best and more about who is asking. Work through three questions in order:

  • What does the law require? Not choices. List them first.
  • What do customers require? Read contracts, lost deals and questionnaires. US buyers often ask for SOC 2; European buyers and public tenders often ask for ISO/IEC 27001.
  • What does the business need? A voluntary framework to structure and report on the program.

Then sequence. The scheduling company's US pipeline keeps asking for SOC 2, and a European hospital group wants ISO/IEC 27001 next year. It builds one control set against both and its legal obligations, takes the SOC 2 examination first because that unblocks the most revenue, then pursues certification on the same controls and evidence.

6Crosswalks and the common control set

A crosswalk (or mapping) records which requirements in one framework correspond to which in another. Built around the organization's own controls, it becomes a common control set: each control written, owned and tested once, and mapped to every requirement it answers.

The type of each mapping matters. Multi-factor authentication fully answers a requirement to verify who is seeking access, but only partly answers SOC 2's logical access criterion, which also expects access to be granted, removed and reviewed. Recording that MFA covers CC6.1, without "partially", leaves a gap an auditor will find. The usual relationships:

  • Equivalent: the control and the requirement ask for the same thing.
  • Partial: the control covers part of the requirement; other controls cover the rest.
  • Broader: the control does more than the requirement asks.
  • Related: the topics overlap, but neither satisfies the other.

Mappings also age. ISO/IEC 27001's move to the 2022 edition renumbered and regrouped Annex A; CSF 2.0 added a function. Each mapping should record the edition it points to, its owner and its last review, so a framework update becomes a planned review rather than a surprise.

Meta-frameworks such as the Secure Controls Framework publish a control set already mapped to many laws and frameworks; some programs start from one. The mappings remain the organization's to check.

7In practice: build a one-page crosswalk

Forty-five minutes and a spreadsheet.

  1. Sort your obligations. List every law, contract and framework that applies, and label each must, agreed or chosen.
  2. Pick three controls you already run, such as MFA, backups and access reviews. Write each as one sentence with an owner.
  3. Map each control to the matching requirement in at least three of your obligations. Mark every mapping as equivalent, partial, broader or related, and note the edition you mapped to.
  4. Find the gaps. For every partial mapping, name the control that covers the rest. If there is none, you found the gap before an auditor did.

8Key terms

Certification
An accredited certification body's formal statement that a management system, within a stated scope, meets a standard such as ISO/IEC 27001.
Attestation
An independent auditor's examination and written opinion on an organization's controls against published criteria, as in a SOC 2 report.
Statement of Applicability
The ISO/IEC 27001 record of which Annex A controls the organization applies, which it excludes, and why.
Trust Services Criteria
The AICPA's criteria for SOC 2, grouped into security (always in scope), availability, processing integrity, confidentiality and privacy.
Crosswalk
A recorded mapping between frameworks' requirements, or between an organization's controls and those requirements.
Common control set
One set of owned controls, each mapped to every requirement it answers, so that work and evidence are done once and reused.
